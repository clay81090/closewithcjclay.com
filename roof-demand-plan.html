0.3 The one-paragraph version

If you read nothing else

Three Florida mechanisms push property owners toward roof work on a schedule: homeowners whose roofs hit fifteen years and receive insurance non-renewal notices, homeowners sitting in a $405 million state grant queue, and condominium associations legally barred from underfunding their roof reserves. All three have deadlines, defined scopes of work, and no one telling them who to call. We find them using public records joined to private signals from an insurance and inspection network already in place, route them to contractors who pay a setup fee and a monthly retainer for the system, and take a flat fee per square on jobs we source. One contractor account is worth $2,000–$4,000 a month before a single sourced job, and the whole thing runs at roughly 95% gross margin.

Part 03

Roofing, from zero

Everything after this assumes this section. No prior knowledge needed.

3.1 How a roof job actually works

  1. Something triggers it. A leak, a storm, a home sale, an insurance letter, a grant. The trigger determines who pays and how fast.
  2. Inspection. Somebody gets on the roof or flies a drone.
  3. Measurement. Roofs are measured in squares — one square = 100 square feet. A typical Florida house is 20–35 squares. Usually measured by aerial imagery (EagleView) rather than a tape measure.
  4. Quote. Material, tear-off, underlayment, decking repair, permit, disposal. Jobs are won or lost here, especially on premium products.
  5. Signature and deposit. Florida gives a standard three-business-day right of cancellation, so a signature isn't final until that passes and money moves.
  6. Permit. Pulled with the local building department — and it's public record, which is why permit data matters so much to us later.
  7. Material order. Through a distributor: ABC Supply, Beacon, Gulf Eagle.
  8. Build. Usually a subcontracted crew, not employees. One to three days on a typical re-roof.
  9. Final inspection and payoff.

The full cycle runs three weeks to three months on residential, longer with insurance involved, six months or more on large commercial. Hold onto that — it drives the entire pricing decision in Part 08.

Part 04 · Demand Engine 1

The 15-year roof clock

The best of the three, because it never expires — roofs get older every single day, and the law turns a specific birthday into a forced decision with a deadline attached.

4.1 The statute, quoted

Florida Statute §627.7011(5) governs what an insurer may do about roof age. Three sentences matter FACT — Florida Statutes, 2025:

“An insurer may not refuse to issue or refuse to renew a homeowner's policy insuring a residential structure with a roof that is less than 15 years old solely because of the age of the roof.”

“For a roof that is at least 15 years old, an insurer must allow a homeowner to have a roof inspection performed by an authorized inspector at the homeowner's expense before requiring the replacement of the roof.”

“The insurer may not refuse to issue or refuse to renew a homeowner's insurance policy solely because of roof age if an inspection of the roof … indicates that the roof has 5 years or more of useful life remaining.”

Translation

Under fifteen years, roof age alone cannot cost a homeowner their insurance. At fifteen, that protection ends. The insurer can now demand replacement as a condition of coverage — but must first allow a paid inspection. If that inspection shows five or more years of useful life, the insurer has to keep them on roof age alone.

So a roof's fifteenth birthday converts a passive homeowner into someone who must decide, spend money, and do it on a clock.

4.2 What actually happens

  1. The carrier's system flags the policy. Roof age is a field in the underwriting record. Crossing fifteen years pulls the account for review automatically. This is software, not a human judgment — which is exactly why it's predictable.
  2. A non-renewal notice goes in the mail. Florida requires 120 days' advance written notice FACT. The homeowner opens an envelope saying coverage ends in four months.
  3. They call their insurance agent. Not a roofer. The agent. This is the single most important behavioural fact in this document.
  4. The agent has the same problem. Policy lapses, commission gone, client probably gone with it. Their interest and ours already point the same way.
  5. The agent calls someone who can fix it. Which is already happening — see 4.5.

4.3 The 2024 change that helps

Florida House Bill 1611, effective July 2024, expanded who may perform the certifying inspection to include a licensed roofing contractor ESTIMATE — reported by a Florida insurance law firm; confirm the statutory text before using it in a client-facing claim.

Operationally useful: the contractor we route to can perform the inspection that determines whether replacement is needed at all. It's also a place to be scrupulous — a contractor who profits from replacement assessing whether replacement is necessary is a conflict the homeowner should be told about. Disclose it, always.

4.5 It already works — that's the important part

Not a hypothesis

One insurance agency in the network already runs a manual version of this. Their carrier's system auto-flags roofs around fifteen years at renewal, the notice produces a call, and that work has been referred out and paid on for years FACT.

We are not inventing a channel. We're systematizing one that already produces money one roof at a time, then multiplying it across the twenty-plus agency locations already reachable. That's the difference between a plan and a rollout — and it's why this is the first call of week one rather than something to build toward.

4.6 The upgrade — run it forward instead of backward

Today it's reactive: notice prints, phone rings, someone scrambles. That catches only the homeowners who call.

An agency's book contains, for every policy, the roof's age, its material and the renewal date. That data is private, sits in their management system, and has never been used as a prospecting asset. Offer a monthly scan that identifies every policy crossing fifteen years in the next 6–12 months and starts the conversation before the notice ever prints. The homeowner gets months instead of weeks. The agency looks like a hero and protects the policy. We get a dated pipeline.

And critically: the scan is worth paying for as a retention tool whether or not a single roof is ever sold. That makes it easy to price, easy to justify, and clean.

Part 05 · Demand Engine 2

The grant queue

Roughly $405 million of state money earmarked for roof and opening work, tens of thousands of homeowners in line for it, and by the program's own design no mechanism connecting any of them to a contractor.

5.1 What the program is

My Safe Florida Home (MSFH) is a state grant program run by the Department of Financial Services that pays homeowners to strengthen homes against hurricanes: roof reinforcement, roof-to-wall connections, secondary water resistance, impact windows and doors.

  • Up to $10,000 per home from the state.
  • Low-income applicants (≤80% of county area median income) receive the full grant with no match required.
  • Everyone else gets a 2-to-1 match — the state adds $2 for every $1 the homeowner spends. ESTIMATE
  • The Memorial Day 2026 budget reappropriated over $405 million across MSFH and its condominium counterpart. ESTIMATE — verify with DFS before any client-facing claim
  • Roughly 45,000 homeowners completed wind-mitigation inspections in earlier cycles but never received an award before money ran out. Those applications are live. ESTIMATE
The gap, verified

The program “no longer maintains a list of authorized contractors or requires their use” for new construction. FACT — the program's own website Homeowners are explicitly responsible for finding and vetting their own licensed contractor, and the program assumes no responsibility for that contractor's performance.

In business terms: the state funded tens of thousands of specific, defined jobs and then deliberately removed itself from connecting those jobs to anyone who can do them.

5.2 Can homeowners just take the money? No.

The obvious objection, from insurance-claim experience: homeowners who received an ACV check (actual cash value, no strings) historically pocketed it and never fixed the roof. If grants worked that way, the queue would be a list of people who want cash rather than roofs.

They don't. Both grant types are reimbursement-based FACT:

  1. Approval must happen before work begins — starting early disqualifies the grant entirely.
  2. A licensed contractor must complete the work.
  3. The work must pass a final inspection.
  4. Only then is a paid invoice submitted and reimbursed.

Structurally the opposite of an ACV check. No licensed contractor finishing real work means no money at all. Everyone in that queue must hire a contractor to get paid.

5.3 So where do people fall out?

Not by pocketing cash — by process abandonment. Every one of these is a service opportunity:

Failure point
Paperwork friction

Multi-step application, income documentation, homestead verification. People stall and never restart.

Failure point
Wrong-trade contractor

They call someone who doesn't do the wind-mit scope or won't handle grant documentation.

Failure point
Missed documentation

Reimbursement requires specific paperwork. Miss it and the money doesn't come.

Failure point
Missed inspection window

The final inspection has to happen and be recorded. Nobody is chasing it.

Each failure is a homeowner with approved state money who doesn't get a roof, and a contractor who doesn't get a job. A concierge who walks them through it is worth money to both sides.

5.4 The condominium version — which links Engine 2 to Engine 3

A parallel condominium pilot program funds associations: roof projects at $11 per square foot of replacement roof, capped at $1,000 per unit and 50% of cost, maximum $175,000 per association, same 2-to-1 match. ESTIMATE — verify current appropriation

This matters because a condo association facing a mandatory roof reserve can partially fund the actual work through this program. On an 84-unit building that's up to $84,000 against the project — and almost nobody calling on that board knows it exists.

5.5 The private signal

Who is in the queue and at what stage is not public. It can't be scraped or bought. But wind-mitigation inspectors see it — they're in the house, they know the scope, they know whether an application is in progress. There is already an inspector relationship in the network.

Why this is the defensible layer

Everything else in the data product is buildable by a competent competitor with a budget — permits, parcel data, sales records are public. Grant-pipeline status is reachable only through a human relationship, with the homeowner's consent. That's the moat, and it's why the inspector conversation ranks above the engineering in the build order.

Part 06 · Demand Engine 3

Condo compliance

The biggest job sizes of the three, on a statutory deadline 132 days out. Also the easiest place to overstate the law, so this section is deliberately careful.

6.1 How Florida got here

  1. June 2021 — Surfside. Champlain Towers South collapsed. Ninety-eight people died. The investigation traced to long-deferred structural maintenance and reserves that had been repeatedly voted down.
  2. 2022 — Senate Bill 4-D. Two mandates land on condominium associations: milestone structural inspections and Structural Integrity Reserve Studies.
  3. 2025 — House Bill 913. Amendment adjusting thresholds, adding flexibility, setting current deadlines. Effective July 1, 2025. FACT

6.2 The two requirements

Milestone inspection

A structural inspection by a licensed engineer or architect, required for buildings three or more habitable stories at 30 years old — or 25 years if within three miles of the coast — and every ten years after. FACT

Why the coastal trigger matters: Naples, Marco Island, Fort Myers and coastal Southwest Florida hit the trigger five years earlier than inland stock, and that region is dense with mid-rise condo buildings. Highest-concentration territory in the state.

SIRS — Structural Integrity Reserve Study

A formal study inspecting eight components, estimating remaining life and replacement cost for each, then telling the association how much to set aside annually. The eight are: roof, load-bearing structure, fireproofing and fire protection, plumbing, electrical, waterproofing and exterior painting, windows and exterior doors, and any other item over $25,000 affecting structural integrity. FACT

The provision with teeth

For budgets adopted January 1, 2025 or later, owners can no longer vote to waive or reduce reserve funding for SIRS components — and the main roof is one of them. FACT

Boards used to routinely vote to skip funding, because nobody wants to raise their own dues. That escape hatch is closed for the roof. The association must budget real money against a real replacement cost — which means it needs a real number from a real contractor.

6.3 The deadline

SIRS was due December 31, 2025, with coordination alongside a milestone inspection permitted only through December 31, 2026 FACT — 132 days from today. The associations that delayed are hitting the wall this quarter and need numbers to build a 2027 budget.

6.4 Who this does NOT apply to

The distinction that keeps us honest

Milestone inspections and SIRS apply only to condominiums and cooperatives under Florida Statutes Chapters 718 and 719, in buildings of three or more stories. FACT

They do not apply to single-family, duplex or townhome homeowners' associations under Chapter 720. Those have no statutory reserve or inspection mandate at all — reserves exist only if a developer established them or members voted them in, and can be waived by majority vote.

Get this wrong on a call and a board member who lives with these statutes will correct you. Confirm the chapter and the story count before saying a word about the law.

6.5 The flexibility provision — don't oversell it

HB 913 created a real escape valve: associations that completed a milestone inspection within the prior two years may pause reserve funding for up to two budget years, through December 31, 2028 FACT. So never tell a board or a roofer that an association is "forced to pay now" without checking that association's specific status. The urgency is real for many buildings and absent for others.

6.6 Who you're actually selling to

The largest apartment manager in the United States runs 288 communities and 85,381 units in Florida alone FACT — verified, and there is a personal contact there plus crews currently working their jobs who can identify the actual decision-makers. That combination — a warm contact plus ground-level intelligence — is not something a competitor assembles.

6.7 Verdict

Time-boxed sprint, not the foundation. Biggest job sizes, real deadline, long institutional sales cycle. Treat it as a Q4 campaign with a hard stop, run alongside the two continuous engines. After the deadline it converts into a smaller permanent annuity — milestone inspections repeat every ten years and reserve studies must be refreshed.

8.4 Where performance-based structures fit

Revenue share is a strong model — it removes price objections from a cash-poor buyer, aligns incentives, and can pay far more than a retainer on a good account. Three conditions make it work well, and where they're met we should use it:

Condition 1
We own attribution

The call routes through our tracking number and the opportunity lives in our CRM. If we can count it, we can bill it. Without that, the percentage is a hope.

Condition 2
Institutional counterparty

A manufacturer co-op program or a management company has accounts payable, approvals and a reputation. Very different collection profile from a three-truck outfit.

The job is big enough

On a $150,000 commercial roof a staged percentage is worth structuring carefully for. On a $14,000 shingle job, per-square is simpler and lands sooner.

The sequencing

Recurring revenue builds the floor first, because a company in its first year can't run on money that arrives in ninety days. Per-square rides alongside, because it pays at deposit. Performance participation layers on where the three conditions above genuinely hold — manufacturer programs and large commercial being the obvious candidates.

That's a sequencing decision, not a rejection. The model is right; the order matters.

The rule governing every system above

Anything that must be right every single time is code, not a model. Routing an active leak. Honoring a STOP. Calculating a price. Deciding whether a record scores 70. A rule that's right 97% of the time is a liability when the 3% is somebody's roof or somebody's opt-out.

Models do the things that were previously impossible at scale: holding a natural conversation, writing a message that references a specific job, reading a reply and understanding intent, turning structured data into a paragraph a human wants to read, translating trade language correctly.

10.1 The five guardrails

Guardrail 1
Human in the loop, early

Every generated message reviewed before sending for the first three clients on any system. The gate comes out after a few hundred real outputs, not before.

Guardrail 2
STOP is code

Opt-out never routes through a model. Regex match, instant suppression, permanent, platform-level.

Guardrail 3
Boundaries in prompt AND check

The voice agent is told never to interpret coverage or discuss deductibles — and a post-call classifier scans transcripts for it anyway.

Guardrail 4
Models never do arithmetic

Every price, score and comparison calculated in code. Models explain numbers; they don't produce them.

Guardrail 5
Fail loud, not silent

A dead API, a short data pull, a low-confidence safety translation — all alert and hold. Shipping a broken list to a paying client is worse than shipping late.

10.2 What we deliberately don't use AI for

  • The first conversation with a contractor. This industry buys from people. An AI-sent cold email is the fastest way to be ignored by exactly the buyer we want.
  • Anything touching a claim. No policy interpretation, no coverage question, no deductible. Escalates to a human, always.
  • Pricing a job. Formula engine, rate card, measurement report.
  • Deciding what's urgent. Rule table.

17.2 Compliance essentials

Florida regulates this industry aggressively, for reasons that are largely deserved. Nothing in this plan requires crossing a line, but several things sit close enough that we should know where the lines are before writing a single piece of outreach copy.

The short list
AreaThe rule
Never adjust or interpretWe can explain that a non-renewal letter exists and what the statute says about roof age. We cannot tell a homeowner what their policy covers, what they're owed, or negotiate with a carrier — that requires a licensed public adjuster. Brightest line in the plan.

Glossary

Florida law, programs & agencies

§489.147
The Florida statute governing prohibited property insurance practices by contractors — solicitation, advertising, inducements, claim handling. Penalties to $10,000 per violation.
§627.7011(5)
The statute containing the 15-year roof rule. The legal engine behind Part 04.
Ch. 718 / 719 / 720
Chapters of the Florida Statutes. 718 governs condominiums, 719 cooperatives, 720 homeowners' associations. Milestone and reserve-study requirements apply only to 718 and 719 buildings of three or more stories — never to a Chapter 720 HOA.
AOB
Assignment of Benefits. A homeowner signing insurance claim rights over to a contractor. Banned on residential property policies as of January 1, 2023 — a major reason Florida roofers are hurting now.
DBPR
Department of Business and Professional Regulation. Licenses Florida contractors. Publishes free downloadable licensee and applicant data files.
DFS
Department of Financial Services. Runs the My Safe Florida Home program.
FTSA
Florida Telephone Solicitation Act. Florida's text and telemarketing law. Aggressive, plaintiff-friendly, and our main compliance exposure.
HB 913
The 2025 law amending condominium milestone and reserve requirements, including the pause provision. Effective July 1, 2025.
HB 1611
2024 law reported to expand who may perform a certifying roof inspection to include licensed roofing contractors.
Milestone inspection
Required structural inspection of condo buildings three or more habitable stories at 30 years old — 25 if within three miles of the coast — repeating every ten years.
MSFH
My Safe Florida Home. State grant program paying homeowners up to $10,000 for hurricane hardening including roof work. Reimbursement-based. No approved-contractor list. A condominium version funds associations up to $175,000.
Non-renewal
The insurer declining to continue a policy at renewal — not a mid-term cancellation. Requires 120 days' advance written notice in Florida.
SB 2-A
December 2022 law, effective January 1, 2023, that ended AOB on residential property policies and eliminated one-way attorney fees.
SB 4-D
The 2022 post-Surfside law that created milestone inspections and reserve studies.
SIRS
Structural Integrity Reserve Study. Mandatory study for condo buildings three or more stories, assessing eight components — including the main roof — and setting required annual reserve funding. Owners can no longer vote to waive funding for these components.
Surfside
The June 2021 collapse of Champlain Towers South. Ninety-eight deaths. The event that produced every condo requirement in Part 06.
Wind mitigation
Construction features reducing hurricane damage, documented in an inspection that earns a premium discount. The link between roof work, grant money and an agent's interest.

The condo world

AMC
Association Management Company. A firm employing CAMs and managing many associations at once — often 20 to 80. One relationship, many buildings.
Association
The legal entity owning and operating a condominium's shared property. The customer for compliance-driven roof work.
Board
Unpaid resident owners elected to govern an association. Approves budgets and major expenditures.
CAM
Community Association Manager. The licensed professional running an association day to day. Usually the person who actually takes our call.
Reserve
Money an association sets aside annually against future major repairs. The roof reserve is the one that can no longer be waived.

Roofing & construction

ACV
Actual Cash Value. Insurance pays replacement cost minus depreciation — a smaller check with no strings, which historically often got pocketed rather than spent on a roof.
Deductible
The homeowner's out-of-pocket share. Florida hurricane deductibles are often 2% of insured value. Offering to waive one is a felony.
EagleView
Aerial roof measurement — exact dimensions, pitch and facets from imagery. The objective third-party number our per-square fee is tied to.
Level 3 contractor
A manufacturer's top installer certification tier.
MDF / co-op funds
Market development funds. Money a manufacturer sets aside to help dealers and installers sell more. A budget that already exists — which is why the manufacturer play doesn't require a contractor to find cash.
RCV
Replacement Cost Value. Insurance pays full replacement but holds back depreciation until work is proven complete. The holdback is why RCV money turns into actual roofs.
Re-roof
Replacing an existing roof, as opposed to roofing new construction.
Square
100 square feet of roof. The industry's unit of measure and pricing. A typical Florida single-family roof is 20–35 squares.
Stone-coated metal
A premium roofing category. Homeowner-funded, chosen rather than claimed, sold on presentation.
Tear-off
Removing the old roof before installing the new one. A big share of labor cost.
